The COSTCO management method

February 1st, 2026

Engineering management is easy. All it takes is providing six simple things to your engineers: context, order, space, tools, connections, and opportunity.

Introducing the COSTCO system.

(No association with the Costco Wholesale Company)

Context

If you have an experienced engineering team then this is the most important role of a manager. When the engineers on the team are capable, then the role of a manager is more focused on giving them context so they build the right solutions than mentoring any specific technical skills. Context represents relaying information on business priorities, user needs, technical background, timelines, and scope. In practice, this means I spend a lot of time in meetings so my team doesn't have to.

In the last quarter, my team took on 20 projects ranging from major refactors to small UI changes. For each, I worked with the product and design manager to understand the context so I can staff the project properly. Who is already involved? What is the planned timeline? What APIs already exist? How does the value of this project compare to another? What user research has been done? Do we have any existing documentation? How does ths fit into the company's larger strategy? Do we have success metrics? I'm not accountable for answering any of these questions, but I am responsible for understanding the answers so I can make project staffing decisions, push for a smaller scope, pull in additional engineering teams, or balance the priorities with our technical roadmap. For example, if the project is large and ambiguous I might reach for one of my staff engineers who are comfortable running teams, making decisions, and getting to an outcome. Or if the design manager plans to staff a project with a designer that works well with one of my engineers then I might ensure they have time to partner.

It's an elegant dance, or sometimes an inelegant dance, but if I'm doing my job correctly then each of my engineers join a project knowing the background for it, who they will be working with, and the role they need to play in order for the project to succeed. We staff projects quarterly, sometimes across organization, and across engineering teams. Every once in awhile the dance steps come together and I enter a flow state. In fact, it's the only flow state I ever enter with management.

Actions to establish context

  1. Stay connected to stakeholders to understand upcoming projects and priorities.
  2. Stay connected with your engineering team to understand who they work best with and which areas they have an interest in.
  3. Decide project staffing based on the above information.
  4. Pass along the context to the engineer as they pick up the project

Order

More often called priority in the corporate world, but CPSTCO just doesn't have the same ring to it.

Order is about ensuring your engineers have a clear understanding of their project priorities. No matter how experienced, it can be difficult for engineers to context switch between multiple projects at the same time. My goal is to provide a prioritized list of projects for each of my engineers and then space to focus on the top priority without interruption, more on that next. My current team is highly experienced and works across 7 product managers, meaning I focus more on providing project priorities than managing ticket priority within a sprint, but the importance of order can apply at any level of granularity. It'll just depend on the team's need.

For the Staff engineers on my team, I have slightly higher expectations for context switching, typically prioritizing one development project and one planning project, but in general the engineers shouldn't need to be switching between development context.

Actions to provide order

  1. Provide each engineer with a clear list of project priorities. Don't expect the engineer to take on more than one project at a time.
  2. Check in with the engineers on a regular basis to ensure they are focused on the top priority and not stretched too thin.
  3. Be comfortable with reevaluating priorities and shifting as needed.

Space

Even with the latest AI coding tools it still takes time to develop a solution. For engineers this might be aligning on an architectural pattern, discussing edge cases with design, or most commonly writing code. Engineers need space to focus on the problem in front of them and engineering managers need to ensure they have that space.

Part of this role is attending meetings so the engineers don't have to, but I actually think engineers should be involved in meetings if the meeting is relevant to the project at the top of their order. Once an engineer is staffed to a project it is no longer my role to provide context. As we're planning it's okay for me to represent the team and the work, this allows me to protect my engineer's space, but once the project is in motion then there's no reason for me to act as a middle man for information that is critical to the project's success. Instead, I've focused on growing my engineer's time management skills and have empowered them to utilize meetings to get what they need instead of attending them blindly.

The most important action I take as a manager to provide my engineer's space is to proactively provide an estimated timeline to my team's stakeholders, primarily the 7 product managers, but occasionally a director of engineering or someone like a security analyst. I can't prevent stakeholders from contacting my engineers directly, and I can't prevent, no matter how hard I try, my engineers from responding. I can though, provide an estimate on when the engineer will be available to work on a project. I've found that if I'm proactive in giving stakeholders a timeline then they'll leave my team alone until the team is ready to take on the project. What this looks like in practice is a loose timeline created for planning at the beginning of each quarter. The timeline never matches reality, and I don't even necessarily want it to, but it does give stakeholders an overview of who is working on what and in what order each of them will be involved in projects. This is enough for the stakeholder to know when to engage and if they disagree with the plan they can always come talk with me.

Actions to give space

  1. Proactively set expected engineering availability with stakeholders.
  2. Stay connected with your engineers to understand how often they are context switching. Reevaluate priorities as needed.

Tools

Data, Logging, research, data science, program management, computers, coding agents, etc...

Connections

Growth opportunities, cross team relationships, smarter solutions, building bridges

Opportunity

Career goals, opportunities to demonstrate skills, visibility, technical challenges, soft skills, work happening in other areas.

See, just six simple things. That's all it takes to manage engineers.
